  - Sediment quantity and type; discharge volume and variability (dams); marine processes; receiving-basin slope and sea level (subsidence); climate; human intervention.

## H2 Paper 2 (9173/02), 18 Sep 2025
**Q1 Fieldwork (Cluster 4) – Flood risk, Hawkcombe stream, Porlock, Somerset (UK).** 24 students. Hypothesis: "Flood risk increases downstream along the Hawkcombe stream." Six sites on a weekday afternoon in September; teams of 4 dropped off by minibus, who took photos and began measuring immediately. Width and depth were measured to calculate the wetted perimeter, with an unweighted tape laid along the bed and one mid-channel depth reading using metre rulers. Afterwards, a questionnaire on residents' flood-risk perceptions near the sites.
R1 OS-style flood risk map: Sites 1–4 along Hawkcombe through woods (upper), Site 5 near Hawkcombe and Doverhay, Site 6 at Porlock village (flood risk area shaded); scale 1 km. R2A photo of Site 1: narrow stony channel, stone walls, cottages and a road beside it. R2B photo of Site 6: wider, shallow-looking riffled channel with trees and fields. R3 questionnaire: residence and age; Q1 visit frequency; Q2 distance from the river; Q3 flood likelihood next year; Q4 concern; Q5 awareness of flood-control and naturalised designs; Q6 trust in infrastructure; all 1–5 scales with 1 = most. R4 width / depth / wetted perimeter (W+2D) in m: Site 1 2.2/0.07/2.34; 2 3.1/0.28/3.66; 3 2.1/0.40/2.90; 4 2.2/0.47/3.14; 5 2.5/0.35/3.20; 6 4.8/2.56/9.92.
- (a) Explain why the hypothesis chosen by the students is appropriate for investigating flood risk along the Hawkcombe stream. [4]
  - Strengths: clear relationship, feasible, linked to theory (Bradshaw), measurable. Weaknesses: "flood risk" is undefined; assumes a linear increase downstream.
- (b) With ref to R2A & R2B, suggest two potential risks to students' personal safety when collecting data at both sites, and explain how these risks could be minimised. [6]
  - Site 1: slippery stony bed, so grip footwear and pairs. "Site 5" [sic, should be Site 6 for R2B]: vegetation and steep banks, so designated access points; insects, so long sleeves and repellent.
- (c) Using R2A & R2B, suggest how physical characteristics of the channel and its location could influence flood risk at Sites 1 and 6. [4]
  - Site 1: narrow, steep, upper course, so short flash floods confined to the channel. Lower site: wider and flatter, so overbank floodplain flooding near settlement. (The SAP again says "Site 5".)
- (d) Using R3, explain the strengths and limitations of the questionnaire survey in providing data about flood risk perceptions. [6]
  - Strengths: closed and pre-coded questions; Q1–2 give background on exposure; Q5–6 cover awareness and trust. Limitations: inverted Likert coding confuses respondents; perception is not hydrological risk; weekday-afternoon timing.
- (e) Use a suitable data technique to represent the changes in wetted perimeter downstream shown in R4. [5]
  - Line or bar graph: x = site 1–6, y = wetted perimeter (m), plus a title.
- (f) In addition to the data collected, suggest other types of data they could have collected to help in their investigation of flood risk. [5]
  - Velocity and discharge; rain gauge data; infiltration; land-use mapping; bankfull capacity and past flood records.
- (g) Students concluded that Site 6 has the highest risk of flooding. Using all resources, evaluate the validity of this conclusion. [10]
  - The flood map, physical data, photos and survey are integrated. Against: only 6 sites; single depth reading and unweighted tape; weekday sampling bias; inverted scales; risk equated with channel size; misses rainfall, infiltration, land use and flood history. Treat with caution (SAP text says "Site 5").

**Q2 Cluster 3 – African urban growth, waste, CC impacts, reimaging, Bosco Verticale.** R5 maps of African cities over 1 million, 1990 vs 2018: far more cities by 2018, especially W Africa (Nigeria), E Africa and the Great Lakes, with 10 m+ megacities (Cairo, Lagos, Kinshasa [?]). - (a) Using R5, describe the changes in the distribution of cities in Africa with a population over 1 million in 1990 and 2018. [4]
  - 1990: few cities, in the north (Cairo) and on the W and S coasts. 2018: many more and more dispersed (Lagos in W Africa, Nairobi in E Africa).
- (b) Compare the municipal solid waste destinations among the macro-regions in 2020 as shown in R6. [5]
  - **SAP mismatched to the resource** (see header): it compares Africa's open dumps (about 60%) with sanitary landfill in Europe and N America. General point: developed regions are more advanced, developing regions dump.
- (c) Explain how recycling could affect the demand placed on natural environments. [5]
  - Less extraction: aluminium saves 95% of energy vs bauxite mining (Guinea); paper reduces logging (Brazil, Canada); plastics reduce oil demand. Limits: processing energy, transport, low rates.
- (d) With ref to R7, explain why the impacts of CC might differ across countries. [5]

- (e) Using examples, explain the economic reasons for urban reimaging efforts. [5]

- (f) Using R8, explain how the urban development shown serves as both a mitigation and adaptation strategy in response to CC. [6]
  - Bosco Verticale (Stefano Boeri, 2014). Mitigation: CO2 uptake, particulate filtering, shading cuts cooling energy. Adaptation: reduces the UHI, insulates, provides biodiversity habitat, builds resilience. Maintenance and scalability are limits.
