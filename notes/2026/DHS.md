## H1 Paper 1 (8834/01), Mon 21 Sep 2026
**Q1 Fieldwork – Access to heat-relief facilities, Chinatown, Boston, USA.** 8 students; facilities run by local community organisations. Hypothesis: "Residents of Boston Chinatown living in areas of greater heat exposure have better access to heat-relief facilities." 4 residential sites A–D; 1–3 pm on a weekday in July. 2 students per site: air temperature (digital thermometer), estimated % shade cover; each walked separately to nearest heat-relief facility and times averaged; questionnaire with first 10 adult respondents at each site available & willing (perceived accessibility). R1 schematic map (setter-made): blocks of dense vs lower-density built-up area, Chinatown Library (H1), Quincy Community Center (H2), Chin Park (H3); sites A (dense, by Washington St), B (dense, W), C (lower density, E), D (lower density, SW); 200 m scale; R2 age of respondents vs resident population: 21–39 = 9 (44.5%), 40–64 = 13 (37.4%), 65+ = 18 (18.1%) [2010 Census]; R3 findings: A 35.2°C, 15% shade, 3 min, 82% 'very/fairly easy'; B 34.8°C, 10%, 11 min, 38%; C 31.6°C, 55%, 4 min, 76%; D 32.3°C, 45%, 8 min, 55%.
- (a) Explain one strength and one weakness in the students' hypothesis. [4] – manageable scale (~1.2 km², 8 students, 2 h); "better access" vague/not operationalised (proximity, affordability, ease of entry).
- (b) With reference to Resource 1, explain the strengths and limitations of the students' choice of the four sites. [4] – variety of built environments, some spatial spread, near facilities (safe, feasible); but concentrated in southern half, all close to facilities, under-represent denser areas.
- (c) Explain the ethical issues that may arise from the students' investigation. [3] – informed consent, voluntary, anonymity/confidentiality; minimise disturbance; avoid culturally/racially insensitive questions (citizenship, ethnicity).
- (d) Explain why the walking time recorded by the students may not be a fully valid measure of residents' access to heat-relief facilities. [4] – young students walk faster/different routes & modes; assumes nearest facility used; access also mobility, awareness, opening hours, capacity.
- (e) Using Resource 3, construct a suitable graph to show the relationship between air temperature and mean walking time at the four sites. [5] – scatter graph (MS image).
- (f) The students concluded that residents living in areas of greater heat exposure have better access to heat-relief facilities. Evaluate the validity of their conclusion. [10] – only somewhat valid: A & D support, B & C contradict; outdoor temp/shade/walking time may not represent exposure/access; non-residents/self-selection; thermometer calibration, subjective shade estimates, age-unrepresentative sample (65+ over-represented), one afternoon, 4 sites in south.

**Q2 Climate change – LGM ice, GDP impacts, uncertainty (Section B).** R4 polar maps: max continental & sea ice at Last Glacial Maximum (26,000–19,000 yrs ago: Laurentide, Cordilleran, Eurasian ice sheets + Greenland, sea ice extending into N Atlantic) vs today (Greenland ice sheet + Arctic sea ice only); R5 map % change in GDP per capita due to global warming (−40% to +40%; declines in tropics – Africa, S America, S Asia; severest −20 to −30% Sahel/N Africa & Middle East; gains in N Europe, Russia, Canada); R6 world map of ocean pH monitoring sites (WMO 2023: concentrated on N Atlantic/European coasts, N America, Australia, Chile; very few in Africa, Indian Ocean, Pacific islands, Southern Ocean).
- (a) Using Resource 4, compare the maximum continental and sea ice cover in the Northern Hemisphere during the Last Glacial Maximum with that of today. [4] – similarities: Arctic sea ice, Greenland ice sheet; differences: 4 → 1 continental ice sheets, less N Atlantic sea ice.
- (b) Explain the role of human activities in accelerating the warming of the atmosphere through positive feedbacks. [5] – fossil fuels → GHG → warming → ice-albedo, water vapour, permafrost feedbacks.
- (c) Using Resource 5, describe the distribution of the projected percentage change in Gross Domestic Product per capita (GDP per capita) as a result of climate change. [4] – low latitudes (Africa, S America) decline; high latitudes (Europe, N Asia) increase; worst −20 to −30% N Africa & Middle East.
- (d) With reference to Resource 5, explain the distribution of the projected percentage change in Gross Domestic Product per capita (GDP per capita) as a result of climate change. [5] – tropical vulnerability to extremes, climate-sensitive agriculture/tourism; high latitudes gain growing season, Arctic shipping; wealthy adaptive capacity.
- (e) With reference to Resource 6, suggest reasons for the uncertainties over the present and future effects of climate change. [4] – uneven monitoring (Atlantic-heavy; few Indian, Pacific, Southern Ocean sites); incomplete science (clouds); unpredictable future emissions.

**Q3 Urbanisation, Indian cities, slums (Section B).** R7 OWID/JRC (DEGURBA) % population urban 1950–2100 by continent (Africa ~47% → ~85%; Asia ~61% → ~88%, highest from ~1970; Europe ~65% → ~78%; Oceania ~55% → ~77%; N America ~57% → ~81%; S America ~57% → ~87%); R8 % change 2005–2020 in urban area vs population density, 11 Indian cities (urban area: Coimbatore ~160, Jaipur ~136, Pune ~83, Kolkata 60, Indore 58, Delhi 53, Mumbai 44, Bengaluru 35, Hyderabad 25, Chennai 22, Nagpur 18; density: Bengaluru ~78, Indore 52, Pune 48, Jaipur 41, Coimbatore/Kolkata 28, Nagpur 22, Chennai 10, Delhi 5, Hyderabad 4, Mumbai −12); R9 photo of slum in Delhi (riverside, polluted water & waste, makeshift metal/tarp roofs, densely packed); R10 % slum houses with feature before/after redevelopment, Delhi: sanitation at home 22% → 87%; piped water 19% → 65%; separate kitchen 19% → 59%.
- (a) Using data from Resource 7, describe the trends of the percentage of population living in urban areas. [5] – all rise; Africa greatest (47% 1950 → 85% 2100); Europe smallest (~13 pts); Asia most urbanised by 2100.
- (b) Using Resource 8, compare the percentage change in the size of urban area and population density across Indian cities from 2005 to 2020. [3] – all urban areas grew (Nagpur 18% – Coimbatore 160%); most densities rose; most cities area > density growth; Mumbai only density decline.
- (c) Explain how cities with a large demand on the environment contribute to contemporary climate change. [4] – industry/energy → fossil fuels; deforestation for expansion; landfill methane.
- (d) Using Resource 9, explain how the characteristics of the slum increases residents' vulnerability to climate change. [5] – riverside → flooding with intense rain; poor materials (metal roofs), packed housing → heat stress.
- (e) Using Resource 10, explain how slum redevelopment has improved the social well-being of slum residents in Delhi. [5] – sanitation (disease, privacy, dignity); piped water (clean, saves time); separate kitchen (respiratory health, less crowding).

## H2 Paper 1 (9173/01), Thu 17 Sep 2026
**Q1 Hydropower in Scotland; DJI GPN (Cluster 1).** R1 map of selected hydroelectric stations, major rivers, land >300 m in Scotland (Highlands concentration: Cruachan 440 MW, Foyers 300 MW, Sloy 152.5 MW, Errochty 75, Fasnakyle 69, Clunie 61, Lochay 45, Mullardoch 37, Glenlee 36, Tummel Bridge 34, Tongland 34 MW; none in NE/central lowlands); R2 stacked bars, sources of electricity generation in Scotland 2010–2020 (GWh; renewables incl. hydro ~10,000 → ~32,000; coal ~14,000 in 2010 → ~0 by 2016; nuclear ~15,000 → ~13,000; gas small; total ~45–53,000); R3 maps of DJI GPN 2006 (HQ, R&D, production in Shenzhen; sales offices Tokyo, Seoul, Singapore; main market China & nearby) vs 2025 (HQ/R&D Shenzhen; production Shenzhen + Malaysia; sales/service offices San Jose, New York, São Paulo, Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Beijing, Shanghai, Tokyo, Seoul, Singapore, Sydney; global markets); R4 table impact of DJI's GPN on USA 2023: direct operations 119 direct / 482 total jobs, US$46.3m wages, US$166.4m output; purchases from US suppliers 213/516, $40.5m, $109.0m; distribution & sale 2,012/4,159, $271.5m, $714.2m; commercial use by US businesses 140,143/459,523, $38.8bn, $115.9bn; ~US$12.1bn taxes.
- (a) Describe the classification of provisioning services based on their availability. [4] – proven vs conditional (economically/technologically recoverable?); hypothetical & speculative (inferred, unconfirmed).
- (b) With reference to Resource 1, suggest reasons for the concentration of hydroelectric power stations in some parts of Scotland. [4] – large reliable rivers, high precipitation, steep relief/head; undeveloped land for dams & reservoirs, few competing uses.
- (c) Compare the change in the use of coal for electricity generation in Scotland with the use of renewables between 2010 and 2020 shown in Resource 2. [5] – coal declines while renewables rise; renewables larger from 2011, >50% from 2017; similar short-term fluctuations (both up 2011–12, down 2015–16).
- (d) Explain why hydropower may not fully replace fossil fuels in developed countries such as Scotland. [5] – demand exceeds capacity, limited dam sites, climatic uncertainty, environmental concerns, local resistance; energy security, sunk infrastructure, political/industry support for fossil fuels.
- (e) With reference to Resource 3, suggest reasons for the continuity and changes in DJI's GPN between 2006 and 2025. [6] – continuity: Shenzhen supplier networks, skilled labour, SEZ, coordination; Asian sales offices; changes: Malaysia production (diversify risk, costs), global sales/service offices (markets, local knowledge).
- (f) With reference to Resource 4, explain the impact of DJI's GPN on USA, a host economy. [6] – jobs, wages, supplier demand, output in user industries, taxes, access to drone tech; negative: competition for domestic firms; avoid overstating national-scale effects.

**Q2 ENSO / La Niña, tropical cyclones, Yellow River Delta (Cluster 2).** R5 NOAA CPC ENSO probabilities (issued Mar 2024) Feb–Apr to Oct–Dec 2024: El Niño ~100% (FMA) → ~73% (MAM) → ~14% (AMJ) → ~0; Neutral peaks ~83% AMJ then falls to ~14%; La Niña rises from ~0 to ~62% (JJA), ~75–85% by ASO–OND; R6 maps of La Niña climate impacts Dec–Feb and Jun–Aug (cool/wet/dry/warm etc. – DJF: wet over Maritime Continent/N Australia & N South America, cool & dry central Pacific, cool W Africa & SE Africa, cool NW N America, warm & dry southern USA; JJA: cool / cool & wet over India–SE Asia, warm over E Australia & SW Pacific, dry central Pacific, cool W Africa, cool & wet N South America, dry SE South America) [? read off small image]; R7A/7B NASA satellite images of Yellow River Delta, Shandong, 1979 vs 2000 (rounded lobe → elongated pincer/hook-shaped spit to SE; river mouth shifted; Bo Hai Sea); R8 table discharge / sediment / delta area: 1979 29.6 bn m³ / 714 Mt / 2,690 km²; 1990 12.0 / 314 / 3,038; 2020 19.0 / 199 / 2,893.
- (a) Using Resource 5, describe the pattern of the forecasted likelihood of the three ENSO phases in 2024. [3] – El Niño falls sharply to negligible by JJA; La Niña rises to most likely from JJA; neutral peaks AMJ then declines.
- (b) Compare the spatial patterns of La Niña climate impacts between December 2023 to February 2024 and June 2024 to August 2024 shown in Resource 6. [5] – both over land & sea; wetter SE Asia, cooler W Africa in both; differ in extent, continents affected, number of cool-and-wet areas, latitudinal spread.
- (c) Describe the atmospheric and surface conditions necessary for the development of tropical cyclones. [6] – SST >26.5°C, deep warm layer; pre-existing disturbance, instability, Coriolis, upper-level divergence, low wind shear.
- (d) Using Resources 7A and 7B, describe the changes in Yellow River Delta between 1979 and 2000. [4] – net growth, eastward progradation into Bo Hai; uneven (retreat N, growth S); rounded → pincer-like; river mouth shifts south, distributary direction changes.
- (e) With reference to Resources 7A, 7B and 8, suggest reasons for the changes in the Yellow River Delta between 1979 and 2020. [6] – discharge & sediment load → progradation, later reduced sediment → land loss (3,038 → 2,893 km²); course shifts; waves, tides, currents uneven.
- (f) Explain the impact of human activities on ecosystem services provided by deltas. [6] – upstream dams, diversion, deforestation alter sediment/water (provisioning); on delta: mangrove clearance, groundwater abstraction, tourism, reclamation affect supporting, regulating, cultural services.
