**Q1 BYD – GPN, states/MIs, EV minerals, hydropower (Cluster 1).** Stem: BYD record sales 8.2 million vehicles. - (a) Explain the challenges limiting the progress towards sustainable development. [4] – economic (costly clean tech, limited capacity); political (short-term national interests, disputes on who pays → weak agreements).
- (b) Using R1, describe the ways by which BYD's GPN connects places within the global economy through flows of materials and capital. [6] – materials/components (batteries, electronics) → plants in Szeged, Rayong, Camaçari → finished vehicles to markets; capital: FDI in overseas plants (Hungary, Thailand), revenue flows back; HQ → R&D → plants.
- (c) With ref to R1 & R2, explain positive and negative impacts that GPNs of TNCs, like BYD, on host economies. [6] – +: jobs/linkages, FDI & infrastructure, tech/skills transfer, export/market access; −: dependence/relocation, limited value capture (profit repatriation, R&D at HQ), competition with local firms, environmental/social costs. Max 3 if one side only.
- (d) Using R2 & R3, explain the influence of states and multilateral institutions on TNCs' operations. [4] – Chinese state support (infrastructure, industrial policy) enables Mega Base scale; EU anti-subsidy tariffs (BYD 17.4% vs 38.1% non-cooperating) alter costs, pricing, location decisions.
- (e) With ref to R4, suggest the economic and environmental impacts that extractive industries have on places. [4] – env: open-pit land degradation/habitat loss, water depletion/pollution, dust & emissions, irreversible landform change; econ: direct/indirect jobs, incomes vs single-commodity dependence, price volatility, weak diversification.
- (f) Explain the limitations and trade-offs of alternative energy sources in providing energy security as shown in R5. [6] – large capacity but site-specific (rivers, topography); trade-off displacement of riparian communities, downstream water; dependent on discharge/drought; high cost → loans/foreign firms. Max 3 if only limitations or trade-offs.

**Q2 Myanmar (Mon State) – monsoon climate, landslide, braided river, weathering (Cluster 2).** R6 climograph Thaton (alt. 27 m; mean 26.4°C; 3922 mm; Jul peak ~906 mm, Feb ~3 mm; temp ~24.8–29.4°C, Apr max); R7 (a) 5-day accumulated and (b) daily rainfall 4–9 Aug 2019 at Thaton, Hpa-an, Mawlamyine near Thae Phye Kone landslide (accumulated: Thaton ~450, Hpa-an ~440, Mawlamyine ~770 mm; daily max Thaton ~165 & Hpa-an ~160 on 5–6 Aug; Mawlamyine ~355 mm on 8–9 Aug; landslide 8 am 9 Aug); R8 aerial photo of landslide, Thalphyugone Village, Ma Latt hill, Paung Township, 9 Aug 2019 (forested steep slope, debris over flooded village; 23 killed per source); R9 Google Earth image of Gyaing River near Mawlamyine (multiple channels, islands/bars).
- (a) Cite data from R6 to describe the temperature and rainfall characteristics of Thaton. [4] – Am; mean 26.4°C; ATR ~4.6°C; 3922 mm; wet May–Oct vs dry Nov–Apr (906 mm Jul vs 3 mm Feb).
- (b) With ref to R6, account for the seasonal rainfall pattern experienced in location such as Myanmar. [6] – NH summer: Asian low, SH high, PGF, Coriolis deflection → SW monsoon over Bay of Bengal → Jun–Aug rain; NH winter: Asian high → dry NE monsoon.
- (c) Using R7, compare the rainfall patterns recorded at Thaton, Hpa-an and Mawlamyine between 4 and 9 Aug 2019. [4] – all rise from ~50–60 mm; Thaton & Hpa-an similar (~450/440 mm); Mawlamyine far more (~770 mm), daily max 355 mm >2× others; anomaly 8–9 Aug. (1 similarity + 1 difference with data.)
- (d) Using R6, R7 & R8, suggest how various factors may have contributed to the mass movement event in R8. [6] – heavy monsoon rain saturates regolith → weight + pore-water pressure (↑ shear stress, ↓ shear strength) → mud/debris flow; vegetation weight; steep slope. 1m factor + 1m shear stress/strength link.
- (e) Using R6 & R8, explain the formation of the fluvial landform shown in R9. [6] – braided river (no mark for naming): monsoon high discharge ↑ competence/capacity, sediment supply from mass movement; dry-season ↓ discharge → deposition of mid-channel bars; flow diverts, splits & rejoins; vegetation stabilises bars into islands; seasonal repetition. ([?] R9 near Mawlamyine looks estuarine/anastomosing; AS treats as braided.)
- (f) Explain the differences between physical and chemical weathering processes. [4] – disintegration without chemical change (pressure release, freeze-thaw, thermal expansion, salt) vs decomposition (carbonation, hydrolysis, oxidation; e.g. carbonic acid on limestone).

## H2 Paper 2 (9173/02), 14 Sep 2026
**Q1 Fieldwork – Community response to CC (pluvial flooding adaptation), Rotterdam.** 15 students; Rotterdam low-lying, intense rainfall/pluvial flooding; water squares, green spaces, permeable surfaces, stormwater storage. Five sites: Water Square Benthemplein, Rotterdam Centraal & Kruisplein, Noordplein, Dakpark Rotterdam, Museumpark. Hypothesis: "The extent of public climate-adaptation measures implemented by the Municipality of Rotterdam is greater than the extent of household climate-adaptation measures adopted by individuals in Rotterdam." Online secondary research on sites; questionnaire (awareness, support, adoption) + environmental data-collection sheet; convenience sampling, 25 people/site (125 total); 11am–1pm on two weekdays in October, cool and rainy; approached people appearing ≥18, verified live/work/study in Rotterdam; features within 50 m scored for perceived effectiveness. R1 Google map of 5 sites within city (red perimeter line; scale 5,000 m); R2 table of public measures (Benthemplein 3 sunken basins ~1.7 million L; Centraal/Kruisplein underground storage of canal overflow, extensive paving; Noordplein trees/planted areas but mostly paved, conventional drains; Dakpark elevated park above shopping complex, vegetation/soil retain water, cooling; Museumpark underground reservoir ~10 million L); R3 photos Benthemplein dry vs wet (basketball/soccer pit, skate basin, amphitheatre; steel channels; shallow basins fill first, deep pit only in heavy rain; 1.7–2 million L); R4A questionnaire (Q1 connection: live/work/study/visiting; Q2 age 18–25…>60; Q3 monthly household income bands €; Q4 heard of "pluvial flooding"/"blue-green infrastructure"; Q5 concern 1–5; Q6 Likert support for water squares, parks, permeable pavements, underground storage; Q7 leading "Should Rotterdam spend more public money … because they reduce flooding and improve residents' lives?" Y/N; Q8 household measures adopted (green roof, rain barrel, replaced paving, vegetation, moved possessions, flood insurance – no "none"); Q9 spend bands; Q10 open "what else should the community do"); R4B environmental perception bipolar sheet −5 to +5 for 9 criteria (visibility, clarity of purpose, effectiveness, maintenance, attractiveness, accessibility, community use, improvement, resilience); R5 mean scores per site – total: Benthemplein +35.2, Centraal/Kruisplein +12.8, Noordplein +3.5, Dakpark +29.3, Museumpark +16.2 (Museumpark: visibility −3.8, clarity −3.0, effectiveness +1.5, maintenance +4.0, attractiveness +4.3, accessibility +4.0, community use +4.2, improvement +3.0, resilience +2.0).
- (a) Explain the suitability of the students' hypothesis for investigating the Rotterdam community's response to CC. [4] – must cover 3 criteria: IV/DV defined (type of response; extent); manageability (15 students/5 sites vs spread-out sites, 125 in 4 hrs); measurable (Likert, count of measures, open Q).
- (b) Explain the limitations of the sampling method used by the students. [4] – convenience bias; 11–1 weekdays under-represents workers; rain reduces people outdoors; location bias (users of adaptive spaces); equal 25/site ignores differing user numbers.
- (c) With ref to R1 & R3, explain how the students could minimise potential risks when undertaking the fieldwork. [6] – slippery basin edges (grip footwear, stay on paths); basins filling in heavy rain (check forecast, observe from raised point); traffic/bicycles between sites; cold/wet (waterproofs); hostile public (pairs); separation (phones, meeting points). Risk [1] + mitigation [+1].
- (d) Using R4A & R4B, explain the strengths and limitations of the survey questionnaire and environmental perception survey. [6] – S: closed Qs codable; Q6 & Q8 match hypothesis; open Q10; consistent −5 to +5 scale. L: jargon Q4; leading Q7; subjective criteria; underground measures invisible (Kruisplein, Museumpark); students' not community's perceptions; English only; no intro/anonymity (income question).
- (e) Explain how students could improve the accuracy and reliability of their data collection procedures. [5] – accuracy: pilot questionnaire; check sheets on site; fixed-point photos/feature counts; stratified/quota/systematic sampling. Reliability: standard training/script/50 m area; descriptors for each score; repeat on different days/times and average.
- (f) Suggest how the 'mean environmental perception scores for Museumpark' in R5 may be represented. [5] – horizontal bipolar bar graph from zero, −5 to +5 even scale, 9 labelled elements, negatives left/positives right, title/axis label.
- (g) Using all the Resources, evaluate the validity of the data collected in determining whether the students' hypothesis about public implementation and household adoption is supported. [10] – L1–3 table. Strengths: R2 storage capacities (1.7M L, 10M L); Q8/Q9 household adoption & spend; 125 respondents. Limitations: perception ≠ extent implemented (Museumpark visibility −3.8 despite 10M L reservoir); sites chosen for known measures; inconsistent quantitative info; self-reported adoption, no "none" option; tenants can't install; non-residents; rainy weekday sample; different units, reservoir vs rain barrel not comparable; household results not provided at all. Improvements: city-wide records, stratified sites, objective indicators, residents only, record tenure, standardised indices. Suggested judgement: limited validity.

**Q2 Plastic waste, slums, CO2–temperature, coastal flooding Bangladesh, aquatic ecosystems (Cluster 3).** R6 OWID share of plastic waste fate 2019: Europe landfilled 44%, incinerated 38%, recycled 12%, mismanaged 6%; Asia (excl. China & India) landfilled 39%, mismanaged 34%, incinerated 19%, recycled 8%; R7 photo Smokey Mountain slum, Manila (makeshift stilt shacks on rubbish dump, laundry lines, children); R8 graph CO2 (NOAA) vs global temperature anomaly (GISS) 1964–2008 (CO2 ~320 → ~385 ppm steady; anomaly ~−0.2 → +0.4–0.6°C with fluctuations, e.g. dip ~1992); R9 aerial photo flooding in Bengal Delta, Bangladesh, April 1991 (river embankment, submerged fields/settlements, boats).
- (a) Using R6, suggest reasons for the variations in the methods of plastic waste management between Europe and Asia in 2019. [6] – financial capacity for incineration/WtE (38% vs 19%); level of development & formal collection (mismanaged 6% vs 34%); governance/enforcement (recycled 12% vs 8%). Reason [1] + comparative data [+1].
- (b) Using R7, explain the impact of multiple deprivations experienced by slum dwellers on SUD. [6] – no waste collection/sanitation (env.); insecure makeshift housing → hazards (social); disease vectors; poverty, lack of infrastructure → lower productivity, public spending (econ.); poor education → informal work, poverty.
- (c) Citing data from R8, describe the relationship between changes in atmospheric CO2 concentration and global temperature anomaly over time. [4] – overall positive (320→385 ppm; −0.2→+0.4°C); short-term inconsistency (1988–92 CO2 350→356 ppm while anomaly +0.3→+0.1°C); temperature fluctuations don't affect steady CO2 gradient.
- (d) Explain reasons for uncertainty over the present and future impacts of contemporary CC. [4] – incomplete understanding of feedbacks (ice-albedo, cloud, carbon cycle); sparse in-situ data (oceans, poles, deserts); measurement error; future emissions pathways.
- (e) Using R9, explain the impacts of coastal flooding on different groups of people and the environment in Bangladesh. [4] – poor households (homes lost, less resilient, displacement); farmers (crop loss, salinisation); fishers (boats/gear); environment (erosion, vegetation/habitat loss, saltwater intrusion, pollutants).
- (f) With the use of examples, explain the impacts of changes in aquatic ecosystems brought about by CC on humans. [6] – coral bleaching (Great Barrier Reef → fisheries, tourism); fish shifting poleward (N Atlantic mackerel); mangrove loss → coastal protection; acidification (Pacific NW US oyster hatcheries); food webs → protein/food insecurity.
