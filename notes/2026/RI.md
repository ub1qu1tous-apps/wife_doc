## H1 Paper 1 (8834/01), 2026, 3 h
**Sec A Q1 Fieldwork – Dengue prevention, Marymount–Shunfu (Singapore).** "Marymount Junior College" students investigate the local community's response to dengue. The target population is about 8,000 residents, roughly half in HDB and half in private housing. Hypothesis: "Within the Marymount–Shunfu area, residents living in private housing are more likely to adopt dengue prevention measures than residents living in public housing." The survey ran in the June school holidays, when dengue incidence is higher and conditions are warm with some rain. Two locations: inside Shunfu Market and outside Marymount MRT Station. Students randomly approached the public and handed out a printed sheet and pen. More than 100 people were surveyed, and screening out incomplete and non-resident responses left 62 valid. (QP section label: "Cluster 2: Fieldwork".)
R1 map of the area of study: HDB block in the centre, private housing to the NW and NE, "Other land use" on the E side. Shunfu Market is at the NW edge next to private housing; Marymount MRT is at the S edge next to HDB. R2A photo inside Shunfu Market (hawker centre seating, mostly elderly diners). R2B photo outside Marymount MRT. R3 questionnaire "Dengue Prevention Survey": Q1 years lived (0–9 / ≥10 / don't live here); Q2 housing type (HDB / landed or condo); Q3 "tick all" 8 measures done in the past month (3.1 removed stagnant water ... 3.8 reported breeding sites). R4 results, private % vs public %: 3.1 58/35; 3.2 55/6; 3.3 29/10; 3.4 65/55; 3.5 39/39; 3.6 81/77; 3.7 71/39; 3.8 32/16.
- (a) Using R1, compare the two locations chosen to conduct the questionnaire survey. [4]
  - Compare only locational aspects seen on the map, with no reasons. Similarities: both are inside the study area, both are near public and private housing, and both are on the edge or periphery. Difference: the MRT is near "other land use", so may draw non-resident workers. Saying "both near roads" scores 0.
- (b) With ref to R2A & R2B, suggest how students could minimise potential risks when visiting both locations. [6]
  - Market: risk of colliding with people carrying hot food, so avoid lunch hours and work in pairs with a lookout. MRT: crowds exiting, so stand on a quieter side. Dengue at both: long sleeves, repellent, avoid dusk. Wet floors and dehydration are weaker ideas and score 0.
- (c) Using R3, describe strengths and limitations of the questionnaire survey. [6]
  - Strengths: one page, short, no unnecessary personal questions, clear purpose, a good spread of measures (home, individual, social), and Q1 screens residents tactfully. Limitations: English only, Q2 may be sensitive (no "rather not say"), no frequency of each measure, no open-ended question, and Q1 makes non-residents complete a survey that is then discarded (ethical).
- (d) Using R4, compare results for public housing with private housing. [4]
  - Private is higher for 7 of 8 measures. Q3.5 is equal at 39%. Q3.6 repellent is highest for both. The MS says the largest gap is "Q3.4 55% vs 6%"; **this is an error, it should be Q3.2 (55 vs 6)**.
- (e) Evaluate the data collection process of this fieldwork. [10]
  - Level-marked; the MS rewards "generative ideas". Sample of 62 is under 1% of 8,000, so aim for 100 or more. June has pros and cons (higher dengue risk but overseas holidays), so spread the timing. Only two sites: the market is biased to housewives and helpers, the MRT misses drivers. Suggests 10 spread-out sites, door-to-door, letterbox drops, or QR online surveys. Adds secondary data (NEA), stakeholder interviews (town council, NEA, Project Wolbachia) and observation (counting people in long sleeves). Data cleaning counts as part of collection.

**Sec B Q3 Climate change – CO2 record, feedback, responses.** R8 graph of atmospheric CO2 and Antarctic temperature over the last 800,000 years. CO2 cycles between about 180 and 300 ppm, and current CO2 is about 410 ppm, shown as a dotted spike. Temperature change ranges from about −10 to +4°C, peaking about 130 ka and about 330 ka. R9 diagram of a deforestation feedback loop: deforestation → reduced transpiration → change in local rainfall → drought → back to deforestation. R10 cartoon (NYT, Chappatte): a man on an oil tank says "People don't grasp the short-term consequences of saving the planet" while protesters hold "Climate change: ACT NOW".
- (a) Cite relevant data from R8 to explain how changes in atmospheric CO2 concentration provide evidence of climate variability. [4]
  - Repeated natural cycles of about 180–300 ppm track temperature. Example: about 400 ka, CO2 was about 280–300 ppm with temperature +2–3°C; lows of 180–200 ppm went with −8 to −10°C. Today's 400–410 ppm is outside the natural range.
- (b) With ref to R8, explain the effects of current CO2 concentration on the natural greenhouse effect. [6]
  - Short-wave in, long-wave out. More CO2 absorbs and re-radiates more infrared, causing an energy imbalance until a new equilibrium at a higher temperature (enhanced greenhouse effect).
- (c) Explain how deforestation affects global temperature through the feedback mechanism shown in R9. [5]
  - Positive feedback: less transpiration, so less rainfall and drought. Less latent heat goes to evaporation, so more sensible heating. Then wildfires, more forest loss and less CO2 uptake.
- (d) Describe how the impacts of contemporary CC may vary. [4] (MS labelled "2(d)")
  - Winners and losers (temperate yields up). Land warms more than ocean. Uneven precipitation (floods vs drought). SLR threatens SIDS more than landlocked states. Vulnerability differs by income.
- (e) With ref to R10, explain the challenges in responding to CC. [3] (MS labelled "2(f)")
  - Short-term economic interests vs the long term, differing priorities of actors, lack of climate finance for LDCs, and poor cooperation on a transboundary issue.

## H2 Paper 1 (9173/01), 2026, 3 h
**Q1 Cluster 1 – Economic sectors, Standard Chartered (TNC), SD, transboundary water.** R1 line graph of employment by sector, 1950–2020. MEDCs: agriculture 22→3%, manufacturing 43→44 (1960)→23%, services 35→74%. LEDCs: agriculture 82→59%, manufacturing 8→15 (1990)→10%, services 10→31%. R2 world map of Standard Chartered: global HQ in London; regional HQs in Dubai, Hong Kong and Singapore; branches coloured by reporting region. The Dubai region covers the Middle East, Africa, Europe and the Americas (US, Brazil, Argentina, Colombia); the HK region covers Greater China, Korea and Japan; the Singapore region covers S and SE Asia and Australia. R3 USGS map of the Colorado River Basin: Upper and Lower Basins, Glen Canyon and Hoover dams, All-American Canal, Lees Ferry, Mexico downstream at Yuma. Caption: very arid; the upper basin is high with a short growing season, the lower basin is low with a long growing season.
- (a) Explain the challenges limiting the progress towards SD for countries at lower levels of development. [4]
  - Limited revenue and capital for green infrastructure; pressure for growth and poverty reduction outweighs environmental rules; weak institutions and corruption weaken enforcement.
- (b) Compare the changes in economic sectors of MEDCs and LEDCs from 1950 to 2020 shown in R1. [6]
  - Agriculture fell in both (MEDC −19 points, LEDC −23 points). Manufacturing fell in MEDCs but rose slightly in LEDCs, peaking about 15% in 1990. Services grew in both, much more in MEDCs (+39 vs +21 points). Maximum 4 marks if only similarities or only differences.
- (c) Describe the spatial distribution of Standard Chartered Bank shown in R2. [6]
  - Uneven. Concentrated in Asia, Africa and the Middle East; few or none in Central Asia and Latin America. The three regional HQs are all in Asia and the Middle East.
- (d) Using R2, suggest how the role of the state may have influenced Standard Chartered Bank's location of regional HQs. [4]
  - Favourable financial regulation, building a hub status, political stability, trade agreements, investment in skills and connectivity.
- (e) Explain the trade-offs in SD of host economies brought upon by TNCs like Standard Chartered Bank. [6]
  - Growth vs environmental damage; jobs vs labour exploitation; tax revenue vs leakage through transfer pricing and profit shifting (relevant to financial TNCs).
- (f) With ref to R3, explain why countries may experience conflict over water resources. [4]
  - Scarcity creates zero-sum upstream (US) vs downstream (Mexico) competition, worst in drought. Upstream pollution and salinity are passed downstream.

**Q2 Cluster 2 – USA precipitation, water withdrawal, flood risk.** R4 map of average annual precipitation in the USA, 2005–2015, in m/yr. The SW interior (NV, UT, AZ, S CA) is 0.0487–0.22; the SE and Gulf states exceed 1.35; the Pacific NW coast exceeds 1.5. R5 map of total water withdrawals by state, 2015, in million gallons per day: CA and TX are in the 20,001–28,800 class; CO, ID, IL, AR, MI, NY, NC and FL are in the 10,001–20,000 class. R6 USGS chart of population and freshwater withdrawals by source, 1950–2015. Surface water rose 140→about 280 bgal/d (1980), then fell to about 198 (2015). Groundwater rose 34→about 83 (1980), then 73–84. Total peaked at about 362 in 1980 and was 281 by 2015. Population rose steadily from 150 to about 325 m. R7 range bars of predicted % change in flood risk in the USA: 2030 is 0–about 50%; 2050 about 10–120%; 2080 about 60–240%; 2100 about 80–290%.
- (a) Using R4, describe the pattern of average annual precipitation. [4]
  - Wetter east and SE (above 1.35 m in MS, AL, GA, FL); driest SW (below 0.44 m in NV, UT, AZ, CA); moderate centre. Anomaly: the WA and OR coast gets 1.18 to over 1.5 m (orographic).
- (b) Cite data from R4 and R5 to identify two states most likely to suffer water shortages in the USA. [4]
  - MS answer: California (withdrawal 20,001–28,800 Mgal/d, precipitation as low as 0.0487 m) and Colorado (10,001–20,000, also very dry). Texas is wetter (about 0.37–0.98 m). [Texas is in the same top withdrawal class as CA.]
- (c) Compare the patterns in groundwater and surface water withdrawals shown in R6. [4]
  - Similar: both rose 1950–1980, peaked in 1980, dipped in 1985, then were broadly stable. Different: surface water is much larger (groundwater about 25–30% of it; 1950 140 vs 30; peaks 280 vs about 85). Surface water rose slightly 1990–2005 while groundwater fluctuated.
- (d) Describe the relationship between population change and freshwater withdrawals shown in R6. [5]
  - Positive overall, stronger 1950–80 than 1985–2005. Population kept rising while withdrawals plateaued (efficiency and conservation). The link is stronger with surface water than groundwater.
- (e) Apart from precipitation, suggest three reasons for the change in flood risk in the USA as shown in R7. [6]
  - Deforestation (less interception and infiltration); urbanisation (impermeable surfaces, shorter lag times); dams (controlled releases or failure); subsidence plus SLR; loss of wetlands and deltas (Mississippi Delta, Gulf Coast); extreme events and storm surge.
- (f) With ref to all resources, explain why the future effects of flooding may vary across the USA. [7]
  - Up to 2 marks per resource. Rainfall intensity and amount (R4); different weather systems (hurricanes on the Gulf and Atlantic coasts, convective storms in the centre); population growth increases exposure (R6); steep mountains bring flash floods while low floodplains flood over wide areas. Hazard and vulnerability are distinguished.

**Sec B [20] (one per cluster):**
- Q3 Evaluate the notion that spatial variations in levels of development are caused by inadequate resources. — Scales (macro-region, country, sub-national); technology, economic and political conditions; flows of trade, capital and labour and global shifts in production, not resource inadequacy alone.
- Q4 'The trade-offs associated with renewable energy sources make them an unreliable solution for energy security.' Evaluate this statement. — Solar and hydro; economic, environmental and social trade-offs vs a reliable and affordable supply; varies by source and context (alternative sources, technology, politics).
- Q5 'El Niño and La Niña will affect pathways more than water storages in the drainage basin system.' Evaluate this statement. — Pathways (runoff, throughflow, infiltration) respond quickly to ENSO rainfall changes; stores (soil moisture, groundwater, channel, snow) respond slowly but for longer, so pathways are affected more quickly, not necessarily more. Spatial variation: El Niño is not simply wetter everywhere; depends on basin soils and relief.
- Q6 'Erosion plays a minor role in the development of fluvial landforms in the tropics.' Evaluate this statement. — High discharge powers hydraulic action and abrasion (V-shaped valleys, gorges, waterfalls); deep chemical weathering supplies sediment; vegetation protects; deposition downstream (floodplains, levees, deltas); upper vs lower course; high-discharge events.
