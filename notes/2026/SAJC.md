## H1 Paper 1 (8834/01), 31 Aug 2026
**Sec A Q1 Fieldwork – Beverage Container Return Scheme (BCRS), Singapore.** Six JC students investigate community response to CC through participation in the BCRS: a 10-cent deposit on bottles and cans carrying the Deposit Mark, refunded at return points. Hypothesis: "Convenience has a greater influence than concern about climate change on people's participation in the Beverage Container Return Scheme." Three shopping centres with reverse vending machines (RVMs), one pair of students per site. Each pair surveyed 30 people near the RVM by convenience sampling, using the same questionnaire, 12–4pm on one Saturday in Aug 2026, and counted RVM users over 30 minutes. The BCRS was still in its transition period, so some containers had no Deposit Mark.
R1 map plus table. Site A: Tampines 1 (NE, Tampines MRT Exit B; RVMs at Cold Storage B1, Don Don Donki L2, Scarlett Supermarket L1). Site B: Waterway Point (N, Punggol MRT/LRT; Don Don Donki B1, FairPrice Finest B2). Site C: The Centrepoint (Orchard, Somerset MRT Exit C; FairPrice Finest B1). R2 two photos of "Return Right" RVMs (one at an HDB void deck beside a Coke vending machine). R3 questionnaire (image): Q1 heard of BCRS (Y/N); Q2 how often used (never / once or twice / monthly / weekly+); Q3 convenience 1–5; Q4 climate concern 1–5; Q5 main reason (deposit / convenient / reduce waste / reduce carbon / other). R4 results by site, A/B/C: mean convenience 4.4/3.2/2.1; climate concern 3.8/3.9/3.7; used BCRS at least once 73/50/27%; RVM users in 30 min 24/13/6. Main reason: deposit 34%, convenience 31%, reduce waste 20%, reduce carbon 10%, other 5%.
- (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of the students' hypothesis. [4]
  - Strength: clear and researchable, with two comparable variables that can be operationalised. Weakness: the scale is too broad ("Singapore") for 3 sites × 30 people.
- (b) With ref to R1 & R2, explain the factors students should consider when selecting sites to investigate how convenience influences participation in the BCRS. [5]
  - Sites should vary in convenience (accessibility, visibility, position in the mall), be comparable in other respects (footfall, mall type, users), and have enough footfall for 30 respondents.
- (c) Suggest how students could improve the accuracy and reliability of the data collected on the number of people using the RVMs. [4]
  - Standardise the definition of a user (deposits at least one container), use a tally counter with a second independent counter, and repeat 30-minute counts on different days to take a mean.
- (d) With ref to R3, explain the strengths and limitations of the questionnaire used. [4]
  - Strengths: closed questions and 1–5 scales give reliability; short. Limitations: self-reported use and social desirability on climate concern; a single "main reason" oversimplifies.
- (e) Using R4, describe the relationship between mean convenience score and participation in the BCRS at the three sites. [3]
  - Consistently positive: A is 4.4 with 73% and 24 users; C is 2.1 with 27% and 6 users.
- (f) Evaluate the validity of the students' fieldwork. [10]
  - H1 fieldwork level descriptors. The transition period confounds participation (no Deposit Mark). No deliberate spread of climate-concern levels (all about 3.7–3.9), so the two factors cannot be fairly compared. Convenience sampling near RVMs, one Saturday and one 30-minute count. Self-report and observation cover different groups and timeframes. Improvements: repeat after the transition period, sample a range of concern levels, and add more sites, days and repeat counts.

- (a) Using examples, explain one tension and one complementarity that can arise between SUD and liveability. [5]
  - Maximum 3 marks for one side. Tension: compact high-density living brings crowding and congestion; Singapore air-conditioning is about 19% of emissions. Complementarity: car-lite design and green buildings; Tianjin Eco-city.
- (b) Using R5 & R6, explain how living conditions in Dharavi present challenges to achieving SUD in Mumbai. [4]
  - Must cover 2 or more SUD dimensions. Social: density and shared sanitation, open sewage, disease (cholera); flimsy materials and fire risk. Environmental: waste dumped by open drains. Economic: deters investment, ill health lowers productivity.
- (c) Describe the population growth rate of Mumbai from 1901 to 2001 as shown in R7. [3]

- (d) Explain why slums develop in urban areas of countries at low levels of development. [5]
  - Rapid rural–urban migration (push and pull factors); shortage of affordable housing and informal-sector incomes; weak governance and planning; high natural increase; marginal land (Mithi River banks, Rio favelas).
- (e) Using R8, explain the challenges in trying to improve the lives of slum dwellers in low-income cities such as Mumbai. [5]
  - Scale and density; tiered cut-offs exclude some residents and encourage new building; politician–slumlord nexus (vote banks); re-encroachment of cleared land.

- (a) Using R9, describe the relationship between changes in temperature and atmospheric CO2 concentration over the past 800,000 years. [3]

- (b) Explain how natural factors may cause changes in global temperature. [6]
  - Solar output; orbital changes (eccentricity, tilt, precession) and northern summer insolation lead to ice-sheet growth; ice-albedo feedback. Maximum 4 marks without feedback loops.
- (c) With ref to R10, explain why human activities are considered the main cause of contemporary CC. [5]
  - Observed warming of about 1.1°C matches human influence; natural factors are about 0; GHG +1.5°C (fossil fuels, CH4 from agriculture); aerosols −0.4°C only partly offset. Maximum 3 marks without R10.
- (d) Using R11, compare the projected global surface temperature change under the three emissions scenarios. [4]
  - All show warming (even low gives 1.8°C); magnitude rises with emissions (1.8 / 2.7 / 4.4); all have a likely range, which shifts upward.
- (e) With ref to R11, explain two reasons why there is uncertainty over the extent of future global warming. [4]

**Q2 Cluster 2 – Typhoon Koppu, Hadley cells, ENSO.** R5 IBTrACS track of Super Typhoon Koppu (Lando), 13–20 Oct 2015 (image says 13–21). It ran west along about 16°N from about 143°E as a depression, became a storm by about 14 Oct, reached Cat 1–4 by 17 Oct, made landfall on Luzon 17–18 Oct, then turned north along W Luzon to about 19°N, weakening to storm and depression by 20 Oct. R6 HKO central pressure and wind: pressure 1002 hPa (13 Oct) to 925 hPa (17 Oct, 12–18h), back to 1002 by 21 Oct; wind 13 m/s to 57 m/s and back to 13; marked "18 Oct crossed Luzon". - (a) With ref to R5, describe the changes in the location and intensity of Typhoon Koppu from 13 to 20 October 2015. [5]
  - Moved west about 144°E→122°E along about 16°N, then north along W Luzon to about 19°N. Intensified from depression and storm to Cat 4 near landfall, then weakened. Maximum 3 marks if only location or only intensity.
- (b) Compare the changes in estimated minimum central pressure and maximum surface wind speed shown in R6. [5]
  - Inverse movement; slow change from 13 to 15 Oct, rapid from 15 to 17 Oct; extremes coincide on 17–18 Oct (925 hPa, about 58 m/s); after landfall the pattern reverses back to about 1000 hPa and 13 m/s.
- (c) Explain the conditions necessary for tropical cyclone formation. [5]
  - SST above 26.5°C with instability; high humidity and latent heat; a pre-existing disturbance; low wind shear; upper-level outflow (divergence); Coriolis (more than 5° from the equator).
- (d) Explain the role of surplus heat in driving atmospheric circulation in the Hadley cells. [5]
  - Equatorial heat surplus drives convection (ITCZ low) with latent heat; poleward flow aloft, subsidence at 30° (subtropical high); trade winds return; the cell migrates with the overhead sun.
- (e) Describe the differences between the El Niño and La Niña conditions shown in R7. [4]
  - Central and E Pacific SST is warm under El Niño, cool under La Niña; W Pacific the opposite. Central Pacific rainfall is wet under El Niño, dry under La Niña; W Pacific the opposite. Approximate lat/long bounds are cited.
- (f) With ref to R7, explain how El Niño and La Niña conditions influence rainfall variations in the tropics. [6]
  - El Niño: weaker trades, warm water spreads east, convection shifts to the central and E Pacific, W Pacific subsidence and drought. La Niña: stronger trades and upwelling, warm pool in the west, wetter W Pacific; interannual variation every 5–7 years.

## H2 Paper 2 (9173/02), 15 Sep 2026
**Q1 Fieldwork (Cluster 4) – Community response to heatwaves, Bangkok.** Six Singapore JC students on an exchange. Hypothesis: "People who perceive heatwaves as a greater risk are more likely to adopt measures to reduce their exposure to heat." An informal discussion with 8 partner-school students shaped the questionnaire. Sites: Siam Square (A), Lumphini Park (B), Chatuchak Market (C). One pair per site, 30 respondents each by convenience sampling, 10am–4pm on a Saturday.
R1 map plus photos: A is a busy pedestrian shopping area with little vegetation or shade; B is a large park with trees and shade; C is a crowded semi-outdoor market with limited shade; C lies further north. R2 questionnaire: age band; Q2 perceived risk 1–5; Q3 disruption 1–5; Q4 how often activity timing or location changes; Q5 measures (tick all: water, shade, indoors/AC, change timing, light clothing/hat, other); Q6 most vulnerable group. R3 results by site, A/B/C: perceived risk 4.2/3.5/4.4; disruption 3.8/3.1/4.0; often or always changed activities 67/47/73%; mean measures 3.2/2.5/3.5; age mix (B older). By risk score (n): 1–2 (10) 1.6 measures, 20%; 3 (20) 2.3, 35%; 4 (34) 3.1, 62%; 5 (26) 3.8, 81%. Most common measures: drink water 82%, shade 69%, indoors/AC 61%, change time 44%, reduce outdoor activity 38%.
- (a) Explain one strength and one limitation of the students' hypothesis. [4]
  - Measurable and clearly defined variables; but "people" is an undefined and too-broad population for 90 respondents.
- (b) With ref to R1, explain two considerations students should take into account when selecting sites. [4]
  - Contrasting heat exposure and shade; enough footfall and range of users; spatial spread (C north, A and B south).
- (c) Describe how students could use convenience sampling to select respondents at each site and explain one limitation. [4]
  - Approach the nearest available, willing person with no predetermined rule, until 30 are done. Limitation: biased to those present (Saturday 10–4).
- (d) Suggest two ways students could reduce risks to themselves. [4]
  - Shade, hydration and rest at A and C; stand aside from pedestrian flows in crowds.
- (e) With ref to R2, explain the strengths and limitations of the questionnaire used to collect primary data. [6]
  - Strengths: Q2 and Q5 measure both variables directly; standardised across sites; "Other" option; closed questions. Limitations: no residency screening (tourists at Siam and Chatuchak); undefined middle points on scales; Q5 lacks frequency; Q6 irrelevant; no Thai version (must be linked to Bangkok, not a generic "language barrier").
- (f) Using R3, represent the relationship between perceived heatwave risk and mean number of measures adopted in a suitable graphical form. [4]
  - Line or column graph (1 mark), axes and plotting of 1.6/2.3/3.1/3.8 (2 marks), title (1 mark).
- (g) Using R3, analyse the relationship between perceived heatwave risk and the measures taken during heatwaves. [4]
  - Positive with no reversal: measures 1.6→3.8; often/always changed 20→81%; largest jump between scores 3 and 4 (+27 points).
- (h) Evaluate the validity of the students' fieldwork in determining the relationship between perceived heatwave risk and community responses to heatwaves in Bangkok. [10]
  - Relevance of the scales; consistency across 3 contrasting sites. Against: convenience sampling, one Saturday 10–4, no residency check, undefined scale points. Improvements: systematic sampling, more sites, days and times, residency screening, defined scales.

- (e) Explain how shrinking global ice cover can amplify the rate of global CC. [4]
