- (a) Describe the global pattern of uranium production among the countries shown in Resource 1A. [4] – very uneven; Kazakhstan dominant; Canada/Australia/Namibia 2nd tier; sharp drop-off.
- (b) With reference to Resources 1A and 1B, suggest reasons why uranium contributes more significantly to GDP in some countries than others. [4] – diversification, dependence on extractive industries, size of economy (Namibia 4% vs Kazakhstan 1% despite less output), other sectors (Kazakh oil & gas).
- (c) With reference to Resource 2, explain how the production and trade of uranium create interdependence between Niger and France. [4] – uranium flow (15% of French imports); French capital/technology (Orano at Arlit); mutual dependence, disruption risk.

- (e) Using Resource 3, describe the spatial organisation of Orano, a French energy TNC that sources uranium from around the world. [6] – HQ France; extraction dispersed in resource-rich countries; partners/host govts; processing in France/Europe; customers in Europe.
- (f) Explain the factors that may have contributed to Orano's spatial organisation. [6] – resource location, comparative cost, history (SOMAÏR since 1968), host-govt agreements, market access, agglomeration in France, GPN specialisation.

**Q1 Fieldwork – Community gardens & sustainable food practices, District 5, Detroit.** Wayne State University students; vacant lots → community gardens (Keep Growing Detroit, mainly District 5) – food security, food miles, food waste, resilience to CC. Hypothesis: 'Residents of Detroit who participate in community gardens are more likely to adopt sustainable food practices.' Permission from garden coordinators; one Saturday 9am–12pm; interviewed 8 residents actively gardening; participant observation (planting, composting, harvesting; field notes; photos of compost bins, rainwater harvesting, shared plots). R1 map of urban agriculture in Detroit by council district 1–7 (community gardens + large-scale farms >1 acre; District 5 mixed income); R2 interview extracts (A 62 retired, groceries closed, stretches pension; B 34 cleaner, food expensive; C 27 started composting; D 55 unemployed, wastes less; E 40 food-price security; F 19 local food better than imports; H 47 "don't think about climate change"; I 31 learned rainwater collection & composting); R3 participant-observation notes + photos (18 residents at raised beds, no vegetables discarded; 4 labelled compost bins, no inorganic waste; 2 rainwater barrels used instead of tap; free food-sharing shelf "Take what you need, leave what you can").
- (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of the students' hypothesis. [4] – testable IV/DV, manageable scale; "sustainable food practices" broad.
- (b) With reference to Resource 1, explain why the chosen sites are suitable for the investigation. [4] – many gardens & farms in D5; spread across district; mixed-income residents.
- (c) Identify three ethical issues that may arise from conducting the participant observation and suggest how the students can address these issues. [6] – informed consent (tags, coordinators inform), photos (permission, blur faces), confidentiality.
- (d) Explain the value of conducting participant observations with the interviews. [5] – triangulation, actual behaviour vs recall/social desirability, context, unreported practices.
- (e) Using Resources 2 and 3, describe the residents' attitudes towards sustainable food practices. [6] – largely positive, mixed motives (cost, food security vs waste reduction); observation confirms; community sharing.
- (f) The students wanted to do an additional survey to support their research findings. Suggest how they could use systematic sampling to select their survey respondents. [5] – location, interval (e.g. 500/50 = every 10th), every nth person/house.
- (g) With reference to all the Resources, evaluate the validity of the students' fieldwork. [10] – triangulation & relevant site (+); 8 interviewees, selection bias (only gardeners), one morning, observer effect (–).
